Mindfulness of Breathing: A Gentle Practice for Beginners

Learning to notice the natural breath, return to the present moment and cultivate patience through a simple meditation practice.

Select Read Aloud to listen to the article.

Have you ever sat down to rest, only to discover that your mind is still busy thinking about yesterday, worrying about tomorrow or planning what to do next?

This is a familiar experience. Our minds naturally move between memories, expectations, feelings and ideas. Mindfulness of breathing offers a simple way to practise noticing where our attention is and gently returning it to one present experience: the breath.

In Buddhist meditation, mindfulness of breathing is commonly known by the Pāli term ānāpānasati. It is an established meditation practice described in early Buddhist teachings and used in several Buddhist meditation traditions, including the Pa-Auk tradition.

The essential idea: We do not need to force the breath to become special or make every thought disappear. We begin by learning to notice the breathing process with patience and care.

1. What Is Mindfulness of Breathing?

Mindfulness of breathing involves bringing attention to the natural process of breathing and learning to recognise it clearly. We may notice the beginning and ending of an inhalation, the exhalation, or the changing sensations associated with the breath.

The practice is not simply about taking deep breaths to relax. In Buddhist meditation, mindfulness of breathing can be developed as part of a broader path of mental cultivation. Its purpose and method depend on the instructions and training being followed.

For beginners, a short introductory exercise can help us become familiar with directing attention to the breath. More systematic practice may involve specific instructions from a meditation teacher.

2. Preparing for a Comfortable Practice

We do not need a special room, expensive equipment or perfect silence to begin. A reasonably quiet place and a posture that allows us to remain comfortable and alert are often sufficient for a brief introductory exercise.

Choose a suitable posture

We may sit on a meditation cushion, a stable chair or another suitable seat. Keep the body reasonably upright without forcing the back into a rigid position. Allow the shoulders and hands to rest comfortably.

Sitting cross-legged is not compulsory. If sitting on the floor is uncomfortable, a chair is a perfectly reasonable option for a simple introductory practice.

Allow the breath to remain natural

We do not need to take exaggerated breaths or hold the breath. Let the body breathe naturally. If attention to breathing makes us feel uncomfortable, strained or anxious, we can stop the exercise, open our eyes and return to ordinary activity.

3. A Simple Introductory Breathing Exercise

The following is a general introduction to noticing the breath. It is not a complete set of instructions for every stage of formal ānāpānasati or Pa-Auk meditation.

  1. Settle into a comfortable position. Take a moment to feel the support beneath you. Let your hands rest naturally and allow your posture to be alert but not tense.
  2. Notice that breathing is already happening. There is no need to create a special breathing pattern. Simply become aware of the fact that the body is breathing.
  3. Notice a natural breathing sensation. You might notice sensations around the nostrils or upper lip, or another aspect of breathing that is easy to observe. Follow the instructions of your teacher if you are learning a specific method.
  4. Recognise when the mind wanders. You may begin thinking about work, food, family, plans or something that happened earlier. When you notice this, acknowledge it gently.
  5. Return to the breath. Bring attention back to the breathing sensation you were observing. There is no need to criticise yourself for having been distracted.
  6. Finish gently. After a few minutes, become aware of the surrounding sounds and the body. Open your eyes if they were closed, and return to your activities at a comfortable pace.
A friendly reminder: The purpose of this introductory exercise is to learn and explore, not to judge ourselves. We can begin with a few comfortable minutes and adjust according to our needs.

4. What Should We Do When Thoughts Arise?

One of the most common questions from beginners is: “Why do I keep thinking when I am trying to meditate?”

Thoughts are a normal part of experience. During meditation, we may become more aware of how frequently the mind moves from one subject to another. Remembering an appointment, replaying a conversation or imagining a future event does not mean that we are incapable of learning meditation.

In this simple exercise, when we recognise that attention has moved away from the breath, we can gently return. We do not have to argue with every thought or become frustrated because thoughts have appeared.

With practice, we may become more familiar with the patterns of our attention. Experiences differ, however, and we should not expect the mind to become calm in exactly the same way every time we sit.

5. Is Mindfulness of Breathing the Same as Deep Breathing?

No. Deep breathing exercises deliberately change the way we breathe. Mindfulness of breathing, in the introductory practice described here, involves noticing the natural breathing process without deliberately controlling it.

Some relaxation exercises use slow or deep breathing for a particular purpose. Those exercises can be different from traditional Buddhist meditation instructions. It is helpful to understand which practice we are doing rather than treating all breathing exercises as identical.

In formal Buddhist training, follow the method taught by your qualified instructor rather than adding extra techniques or changing the breathing pattern without guidance.

6. How Does the Practice Relate to the Pa-Auk Tradition?

Mindfulness of breathing is one of the meditation subjects associated with the Pa-Auk tradition. Within this tradition, concentration is cultivated systematically, and practitioners may receive detailed instructions about how to develop the practice.

A short introductory exercise should not be confused with the complete training. Formal instructions may be more specific than simply paying general attention to the breath. Practitioners who wish to follow the Pa-Auk method should learn from a qualified teacher familiar with that tradition.

In the wider Buddhist path, meditation is supported by ethical conduct and the cultivation of wisdom. The purpose is not limited to obtaining a pleasant or relaxed experience.

7. Bringing Awareness into Everyday Activities

A formal sitting practice gives us time to train our attention. We can also use ordinary activities as reminders to pause and become aware of what we are doing.

For example, before beginning a busy task, we might take a moment to notice that we are breathing and feel our feet resting on the floor. When waiting in a queue, we might notice our posture rather than immediately reaching for our phone.

If a disagreement arises, we may recognise the first signs of irritation and pause before speaking. This does not mean ignoring a problem or suppressing our feelings. It means creating an opportunity to respond more thoughtfully.

These everyday pauses are not a replacement for formal meditation. They are simple ways of remembering awareness in the middle of ordinary life.

8. Common Difficulties and Gentle Responses

“I cannot concentrate.”

Attention naturally wanders. For this introductory exercise, notice when it happens and return to the breath without demanding perfect concentration.

“I feel restless.”

Consider whether the posture is comfortable and whether a shorter session would be more suitable. You may also practise gentle walking or take a break. Formal training may require advice from your teacher about how to work with restlessness.

“I do not feel peaceful afterwards.”

A quiet or pleasant experience is not guaranteed. Some days we may feel calm; on other days we may notice tiredness, thoughts or discomfort. We do not need to force a particular result. If meditation repeatedly brings distress or makes existing symptoms worse, stop and seek appropriate support.

“How long should I meditate?”

For an introductory exercise, a few minutes may be enough to become familiar with the practice. There is no need to increase the duration quickly. If you are following a structured meditation course or retreat, use the guidance provided by your teacher.

9. A Gentle Reflection

The breath reminds us of something simple: experience is happening in the present. Our attention may move away, but we can notice that movement and begin again.

We do not have to turn every moment into a meditation session. Nor should we use meditation as a way to avoid our responsibilities or difficult conversations. Rather, we can learn to make room for awareness while continuing to live responsibly and care for the people around us.

Questions for reflection

  • Can we set aside a few quiet minutes to notice the natural breath?
  • What usually draws our attention away from the present moment?
  • When our mind wanders, can we respond with patience rather than criticism?
  • Is there one daily activity during which we could practise being more aware?

Closing: Begin Gently, Continue Patiently

Mindfulness of breathing offers an accessible introduction to Buddhist meditation. By learning to notice the natural breath, we can explore how attention moves and practise returning to a chosen experience.

This simple exercise is only an introduction. The more systematic development of ānāpānasati and concentration requires appropriate instruction, particularly for those following a specific Buddhist meditation tradition.

We can approach the practice without rushing, comparing ourselves with others or demanding that every sitting feel the same. With patience, we can continue learning about our minds and bring greater care to the way we live.

May we cultivate a steady mind, a kind heart and a clearer understanding of our experiences.
