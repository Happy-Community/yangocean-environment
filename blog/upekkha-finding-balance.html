Upekkhā: Finding Balance in a Changing World

Learning to meet life's changing circumstances with a steady mind, compassion and wisdom.

What Is Upekkhā?

In Buddhist teachings, upekkhā is commonly translated as equanimity. It is the ability to maintain a balanced mind amid changing circumstances, whether we encounter praise or criticism, success or disappointment, pleasure or difficulty.

Equanimity does not mean that we have no feelings, that we do not care about others, or that we should remain silent when something harmful happens. Rather, it helps us respond without being completely carried away by strong emotions, attachment or aversion.

When our minds are balanced, we can see situations more clearly and consider what response would be wise and beneficial.

Life Is Always Changing

Our circumstances do not remain the same forever. People may praise us today and criticise us tomorrow. Plans may succeed or fail. People we love may agree with us at one moment and disagree with us at another. Even our own moods and opinions change.

If we depend entirely on receiving praise, getting what we want, or having everyone agree with us, our peace of mind becomes fragile. We may spend much of our time trying to control things that are not fully within our control.

Upekkhā encourages us to recognise change without immediately reacting with excessive excitement, disappointment, anger or fear.

Equanimity Is Not Indifference

Sometimes, a calm person may be misunderstood as someone who does not care. But genuine equanimity can exist alongside kindness and concern.

When someone is suffering, we can offer help. When we see unfairness, we can take appropriate action. When a mistake needs correcting, we can speak honestly and respectfully.

The difference is that we try to act from understanding rather than from uncontrolled anger, hatred or the desire to humiliate someone. We can care deeply while keeping our minds as clear and balanced as possible.

When We Feel Judged or Misunderstood

It can be difficult to remain calm when another person criticises us, makes assumptions about us, or treats us unfairly. Our first reaction may be to defend ourselves, retaliate or prove that the other person is wrong.

Sometimes a response is necessary. However, before responding, we can pause and ask ourselves:

  • What has actually happened, and what am I assuming?
  • Would responding immediately improve the situation?
  • Can I explain my position without insulting the other person?
  • Is there a practical step I can take, even if I cannot change the other person's attitude?

This pause does not mean that we must accept mistreatment. We can set reasonable boundaries, correct misunderstandings and protect ourselves while avoiding unnecessary hostility.

Understanding What We Can and Cannot Control

We can make an effort to speak truthfully, act responsibly and treat others fairly. Yet we cannot completely control how other people interpret our actions, what they think of us, or whether they agree with our choices.

Recognising this distinction can help us use our energy more wisely. Instead of repeatedly worrying about another person's opinion, we can examine our own intentions and actions.

Have we acted with care? Have we considered the facts? Is there something we should correct or learn from? If so, we can take responsibility and improve. If we have acted reasonably, we may need to let go of the wish to control every person's judgement.

Equanimity in Everyday Decisions

Upekkhā can also guide ordinary decisions. When we encounter a disagreement, receive unexpected news, or see someone behaving in a way we dislike, we can pause before forming a final judgement.

We can distinguish facts from assumptions, consider different possibilities and choose a response that is proportionate to the situation.

A balanced mind can also help us avoid wasteful reactions. We do not need to throw something away merely because we feel uncomfortable, nor should we ignore genuine hygiene or safety concerns in the name of appearing calm. We can examine the actual circumstances and make a sensible decision.

In this way, equanimity supports both inner balance and responsible action.

Practising Upekkhā a Little at a Time

We can begin with small, practical steps:

  • Pause before reacting. Take a few natural breaths when emotions become intense.
  • Notice changing feelings. Recognise that anger, excitement and disappointment arise and pass.
  • Check our assumptions. Ask whether we have enough information before judging a person or situation.
  • Focus on our own actions. Do what is reasonable and beneficial without demanding that everyone respond as we wish.
  • Keep compassion in the picture. A balanced mind need not become a cold or distant heart.

We may not succeed every time. The practice is not about forcing ourselves never to feel upset. It is about recognising our reactions and gradually learning to respond with greater clarity and care.

Upekkhā and the Other Three Brahmavihāras

Upekkhā is one of the four Brahmavihāras, often called the four divine abodes or immeasurable states of mind:

  • Mettā — loving-kindness: wishing for the welfare and happiness of beings.
  • Karuṇā — compassion: responding to suffering with care and the wish to alleviate it.
  • Muditā — appreciative joy: rejoicing in the goodness and happiness of others.
  • Upekkhā — equanimity: maintaining balance and understanding amid changing circumstances.

These qualities complement one another. Kindness without balance can sometimes become overwhelmed. Compassion without wisdom can become exhausting. Appreciative joy helps us celebrate others rather than compete with them. Equanimity helps us sustain a balanced attitude without abandoning care.

Together, these qualities offer valuable guidance for how we relate to ourselves and others.

A Small Reflection

When something does not go as we wish, can we pause before reacting? Can we recognise what is within our control, act responsibly, and allow the rest to unfold without adding unnecessary anger or distress?

A balanced mind does not mean that life is always peaceful. It means we are learning to meet life's changes with greater clarity, kindness and wisdom.

May we learn to care without clinging, respond without unnecessary hostility, and remain steady while continuing to do what is good.
