The Quiet Practice of Appreciating Others

Have we ever received a gift, thanked someone with a smile, but later complained that the gift was not good enough? Have we ever acknowledged someone's effort in front of them, only to criticise or belittle it when they were absent?

These everyday situations invite us to pause and look at our own minds. Can we learn to appreciate the kindness behind an action, even when it does not meet all our expectations?

In Buddhist teachings, muditā, often translated as appreciative or sympathetic joy, is the quality of rejoicing in the happiness, goodness and wholesome achievements of others.

It is more than saying the right words. It is a willingness to recognise goodness without jealousy, resentment or the urge to diminish another person's happiness.

1. Appreciating the Thought Behind a Gift

Imagine that someone gives us a simple gift. Perhaps it is not what we wanted, not our preferred colour, or not something we would have chosen ourselves.

We may naturally have our own preferences. However, we can also recognise that someone has taken the time to think of us and offer something with good intentions.

We do not need to pretend that every gift is perfect. We can simply appreciate the thought and kindness behind it.

If we thank the giver warmly but later ridicule the gift or speak contemptuously about the person, we might ask ourselves: What was happening in our mind? Were we caught up in expectations, pride or a habit of finding fault?

Practising muditā encourages us to notice what is wholesome rather than immediately searching for something to criticise.

2. Recognising Other People's Efforts

Not everyone expresses care in the same way. Someone may prepare a meal, help us with a task, organise a gathering or spend time trying to make something useful for us.

The result may not be perfect. The food may taste different from what we prefer, the arrangement may not suit our expectations, or the work may need improvement.

We can acknowledge the effort without pretending that there is nothing to improve. Gratitude and honest feedback can exist together.

Instead of saying, "That was useless," we might say, "Thank you for taking the time to do this. Perhaps next time we could try another approach."

The difference is not simply the words we choose. It is the intention to address a problem without humiliating the person who made the effort.

3. Rejoicing in Other People's Happiness

Muditā becomes especially meaningful when another person experiences success, happiness or good fortune.

A friend receives a promotion. A neighbour's garden flourishes. A colleague completes a difficult task. Someone in our family reaches a goal they have worked towards for a long time.

We can choose to feel pleased for them rather than comparing their circumstances with our own.

Sometimes, comparison arises before we notice it: "Why did they succeed before me?" or "Why do they receive so much praise?"

We do not need to condemn ourselves for every unwholesome thought that appears. Instead, we can recognise jealousy when it arises and gently turn our attention towards appreciation.

Another person's happiness does not have to diminish our own. We can learn to celebrate their goodness without treating life as a competition.

4. Avoiding Belittling Remarks and Gossip

It can be easy to speak pleasantly to someone in person and then criticise them when they leave. Sometimes, we dismiss their kindness, make fun of their efforts or encourage others to look down on them.

Before speaking about someone, we can pause and ask: Is what we are about to say true? Is it necessary? Can we express our concern without being cruel?

Not every discussion about an absent person is harmful. There may be genuine reasons to seek advice, raise a concern or discuss a problem. The important distinction is whether we are trying to understand and resolve an issue or simply trying to belittle someone.

Muditā encourages us to make room for appreciation. Right speech reminds us to speak truthfully and responsibly. Together, they can help us build relationships based on respect rather than contempt.

5. We Do Not Need to Sweat the Small Stuff

In everyday life, many things do not happen exactly as we wish. A gift may be simple. A meal may be imperfect. Someone may forget a detail or make an honest mistake.

If we keep dwelling on every small imperfection, we may overlook the larger goodness in a situation. We may also create unnecessary disappointment for ourselves and others.

Learning to let go of minor faults does not mean ignoring serious problems or accepting harmful behaviour. It means learning to distinguish between what genuinely needs attention and what we can meet with patience and understanding.

Sometimes, the more wholesome response is simply to appreciate what went well and allow a small imperfection to pass.

6. Practising Muditā in Daily Life

We can cultivate appreciative joy through small, deliberate actions:

  • Offer sincere thanks when someone does something thoughtful for us.
  • Notice the effort behind an action, even when the outcome is not perfect.
  • Congratulate others sincerely when they achieve something wholesome.
  • Notice when comparison or jealousy arises, without immediately acting on it.
  • Avoid making someone else's happiness the subject of mockery or resentment.
  • Give honest feedback respectfully when something genuinely needs to change.
  • Remember that another person's good fortune does not make our own life less valuable.

These practices may seem small, but they give us opportunities to examine our intentions and gradually develop a more appreciative mind.

A Quiet Reflection

Before we criticise someone's gift, effort or achievement, we can pause and ask ourselves:

Can I recognise the goodness in this person's intention? Can I appreciate what is worthy of appreciation, while remaining honest about what needs improvement?

We may not always feel appreciative immediately. Old habits, expectations and comparisons can arise. The practice is to notice them and gently return to a more wholesome way of seeing and responding.

When we learn to appreciate others sincerely, we make less room for needless resentment and more room for gratitude, respect and joy.

Appreciating the Goodness Around Us

Muditā reminds us that goodness is not something we need to possess exclusively. We can rejoice when others are happy, recognise their efforts, and appreciate the kindness they offer.

We do not have to approve of everything, praise insincerely or abandon sound judgement. We can simply learn to see people more fairly and respond with greater generosity of heart.

Perhaps a meaningful daily practice is this: when we notice something good in another person, let us acknowledge it rather than diminish it.

May we learn to appreciate the goodness in others and cultivate joy that does not depend on comparison.
