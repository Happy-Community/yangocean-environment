Before We Blame the World, Let Us Look Within

Rekindling kindness, responsibility and wisdom in difficult times.

Select Read Aloud to listen to this reflection.

There are times when we look at the world and feel troubled by what we see. People gather in anger, communities become divided, and sometimes frustration turns into destruction. Buildings that have stood for generations may be damaged or burned. Places that hold history, memories and cultural meaning may be lost in moments of intense emotion.

When we witness such events, it is natural to ask: How did we arrive here? Why have we become so angry with one another? And what can we do to prevent further harm?

Perhaps, before pointing our fingers at others, we can pause and ask ourselves a more personal question:

Are we contributing to the peace we wish to see, or are we adding to the anger we wish would disappear?

1. Anger May Be Understandable, but We Are Responsible for Our Actions

Every person carries different experiences, disappointments, expectations and struggles. Some may feel unheard. Others may feel that they have been treated unfairly. There may be genuine problems that deserve attention, and there are times when people need to speak up for justice, dignity and change.

We should not dismiss their suffering or assume that every person who protests has harmful intentions.

However, there is an important distinction between expressing a grievance and causing harm.

We may speak firmly without humiliating others. We may demand accountability without destroying public property. We may disagree strongly without threatening those who hold different views.

Anger is an emotion that can arise within us, but we do not have to let it dictate our speech and behaviour.

When we allow anger to take control, we may damage the very community in which we and our families must continue to live. Innocent people may suffer, precious heritage may disappear, and divisions may deepen long after the moment of anger has passed.

We cannot always control what happens around us, but we can take responsibility for how we respond.

2. The Dhamma Reminds Us That Hatred Cannot End Hatred

The Buddha taught in the Dhammapada, verse 5:

“Hatred is never appeased by hatred in this world. By non-hatred alone is hatred appeased. This is a law eternal.” — Dhammapada 5, translation by Acharya Buddharakkhita

Read the Dhammapada verses at SuttaCentral

This teaching invites us to look deeply at the cycle of hostility.

When someone hurts us, we may want to hurt them in return. When we feel insulted, we may want to insult others. When we believe that someone is our enemy, we may begin to see everything they do through the lens of resentment.

But does retaliation truly heal the original wound?

Often, it creates another wound, which may then give rise to another act of retaliation. The cycle continues, and more people become trapped in suffering.

The Dhamma does not ask us to pretend that wrongdoing is acceptable. We can recognise harmful actions, seek justice, establish boundaries and protect others while still refusing to cultivate hatred.

Compassion is not weakness. Restraint is not surrender. Choosing not to retaliate destructively can be an expression of strength and wisdom.

If we wish for a more peaceful society, the practice must begin in our own minds, our own words and our own actions.

3. Before Judging Others, Let Us Practise Self-Reflection

It is often easier to identify the faults of others than to recognise our own.

We may quickly see another person's arrogance, dishonesty, anger or selfishness, while overlooking similar tendencies within ourselves.

The Buddha's teaching in Dhammapada verse 158 reminds us:

“One should first establish oneself in what is proper; then only should one instruct others.” — Dhammapada 158, translation by Acharya Buddharakkhita

Read the Dhammapada's teaching on self-discipline

Before demanding that others become patient, are we practising patience?

Before asking others to respect our rights, do we respect theirs?

Before criticising the carelessness of society, do we take care of the places and people around us?

Before blaming the world for our unhappiness, have we examined our own expectations, habits and decisions?

These questions are not intended to make us feel guilty. They are invitations to become more aware.

We cannot expect the entire world to change according to our wishes. But we can begin to change the way we think, speak and act. When more people take this responsibility seriously, the effects can extend into families, workplaces and communities.

Self-reflection is not about blaming ourselves for everything that goes wrong. It is about honestly recognising the part that belongs to us.

4. No One Else Can Live Our Lives for Us

Other people can guide us, support us, encourage us and sometimes hinder us. Our circumstances may be difficult, and some obstacles may be beyond our control.

Nevertheless, no one else can make every decision on our behalf, cultivate our inner peace for us, or build our future in our place.

The Buddha taught in Dhammapada verse 160:

“One truly is the protector of oneself; who else could the protector be?” — Dhammapada 160, translation by Acharya Buddharakkhita

Read Dhammapada verses 157–166

This teaching reminds us of the importance of personal effort and self-discipline.

We may not have chosen every difficulty that comes into our lives. We may have experienced unfair treatment, loss, disappointment or circumstances that seem beyond our control. We should not use this teaching to blame people for suffering that was not their fault.

Instead, we can ask: Given the circumstances we face, what wholesome action can we take next?

We can learn new skills, improve our habits, seek help when necessary, make wiser decisions and cultivate qualities that support our well-being.

We may not be able to change the past, and we cannot guarantee every outcome. But our present choices can influence the direction in which our lives develop.

Rather than spending all our energy blaming others, we can use some of that energy to build a better future.

Responsibility begins when we stop waiting for someone else to do all the work that belongs to us.

5. Understanding Conditions Without Becoming Trapped in Blame

In the Dhamma, events do not arise in isolation. Many conditions come together to produce particular outcomes. Our thoughts, intentions, habits, relationships and surrounding circumstances can all influence what happens.

Understanding this encourages us to look beyond simple accusations.

When conflict arises, we can investigate its causes, listen to different perspectives, identify harmful behaviour and seek practical solutions. We can acknowledge injustice where it exists and hold people accountable for their choices.

But we should be careful not to assume that every event has a predetermined purpose, or that those who suffer must somehow deserve their suffering.

Not everything happens because we chose it. Not every misfortune is within our control. And not every painful experience has an obvious explanation.

Wisdom lies in understanding conditions as clearly as we can, learning from experience, and choosing a constructive response rather than adding more suffering.

Instead of asking only, “Who is to blame?”, we can also ask:

  • What conditions contributed to this situation?
  • What can we learn from what happened?
  • What can be done to prevent similar harm?
  • What can each of us responsibly contribute towards a solution?

These questions do not remove accountability. They help us move from accusation towards understanding, and from resentment towards meaningful action.

6. Let Us Protect What Belongs to All of Us

A community is more than a collection of buildings and roads. It is made up of shared memories, public spaces, cultural heritage, natural surroundings and relationships among people who may not always agree.

An ancient building may represent the craftsmanship, history and lives of generations before us. A public space may provide shelter, connection or recreation. A neighbourhood may hold the memories of people from different backgrounds who have learned to live alongside one another.

Once something precious has been destroyed, it may be impossible to restore it fully.

We can disagree with the decisions of institutions without destroying the heritage and shared resources of the community. We can call for reform while respecting the safety of others. We can stand firmly for what we believe is right while remaining mindful of the consequences of our actions.

Before acting in anger, we might pause and ask:

Will this action help resolve the problem, or will it create another problem for others to bear?

Will it protect the vulnerable, or place more people in danger?

Will future generations see our actions as constructive courage, or as destruction that could have been avoided?

Our conduct matters, especially when our emotions are strongest.

7. Rekindling Kindness Begins With Small Actions

Kindness does not require us to agree with everyone. It does not require us to remain silent in the face of wrongdoing. It asks us to remember the humanity of others even when we disagree with them.

We can begin with simple practices:

  • Pause before responding when we feel angry.
  • Listen carefully before deciding that we understand another person's intentions.
  • Speak truthfully without unnecessary cruelty.
  • Refuse to spread unverified accusations or inflammatory messages.
  • Express disagreement through peaceful and constructive means.
  • Protect public spaces, cultural heritage and the natural environment.
  • Admit our own mistakes and make amends when we have caused harm.
  • Offer practical help when someone is struggling.
  • Teach children through our own example that strength and kindness can exist together.

These actions may appear small beside the great problems of the world. Yet a peaceful society cannot be built only through speeches, laws or demands directed at others. It also depends on the everyday choices of its people.

Every time we restrain an unkind word, refuse to join a harmful act or choose understanding over contempt, we help prevent suffering from spreading further.

A Small Reflection

Perhaps the world does not need more people who are ready to condemn one another. Perhaps it needs more people who are willing to pause, reflect honestly and take responsibility for their own conduct.

When we feel angry, let us examine the anger.

When we feel wronged, let us seek a wise and constructive response.

When we see others making mistakes, let us remember that we, too, are capable of making mistakes.

When we wish for a better society, let us ask what we can contribute towards it.

We cannot command others to become kind. We cannot force everyone to share our views. We cannot undo every injustice or prevent every painful event.

But we can choose not to add needless hatred to an already troubled world. We can cultivate wisdom instead of impulsiveness, responsibility instead of endless blame, and compassion instead of revenge.

Let us remember that our lives are shaped not only by the circumstances we encounter, but also by the intentions we cultivate and the actions we take.

May we learn to stand for what is right without losing our humanity.

May we have the courage to correct what is harmful, the humility to recognise our own shortcomings, and the wisdom to know when anger must give way to understanding.

May kindness grow in our hearts, peace guide our conduct, and wisdom help us build a future in which all members of our communities can live with dignity and mutual respect.

Before we ask the world to change, let us begin with the mind, the words and the actions that are within our care.

With reflection and goodwill,
